Pick what to compare — whole regions, parts of a region, or single countries — and which food category. Each line is what a kilogram (or litre, or piece) of it costs there, worked out so that the same items are being compared from one period to the next: the line moves when prices move, not when a different shop happens to get collected. Prices are always in US dollars here, so a line can also move because the currency moved — tab 4 splits those two apart. Quiet periods are joined by a dashed line; hover any point to see how much data stands behind it.
Two measures. Price level is the period effect of a two-way fixed-effects fit of ln Pitem,t = αitem + δt over the group's (country, leaf) cells, anchored on the mean item effect so it reads in US$ — the item mix is absorbed rather than left to move the line. Periods need ≥ recurring items and items must appear in two of them, so a group with no repeat coverage simply has no line. Matched-item index is the strict alternative: the mean log relative over pairs priced in two consecutive periods, cumulated. Smoothing is a trailing geometric mean over 2 or 3 periods. How expensive is food, country by country?

Matched-basket price level in US$, item by item.
Each country's food prices are compared with the world median item by item — 1 kg of rice against 1 kg of rice, 1 litre of milk against 1 litre of milk — and then averaged. A country at 150 pays roughly 50% more in US dollars for the same shopping basket than the typical country here. Click any bar to open that country.
Jevons index: exp( medianleaves ln( Pc,leaf,unit / Pworld,leaf,unit ) ) × 100, over COICOP leaf × unit cells with n ≥ 3 observations, retail sources only, unflagged. Countries need + matched leaves, ≥2 sources and <20% flagged cells to be ranked. There are no expenditure weights — this is a price level, not a cost-of-living index.

How much of a price move is the exchange rate?

Indexed to the first period with data = 100.
When a price in US dollars rises, two different things can be happening: the shop really raised its price, or the local currency lost value against the dollar. The local currency line is the shop; the exchange rate line is the currency. The US$ line is what the two do together.
Exact identity in logs: Δln PUSD = Δln Plocal − Δln FX, where FX is local currency per US$. The decomposition below splits the window's total US$ move into the local-price part and the FX part; they add up exactly.
