How have retail prices moved?

Pick what to compare — whole regions, parts of a region or single countries — for the category selected in the tree above. Each line answers one question: how much more expensive is this than it was a year ago? Only items priced in both periods in the same country count, and each one carries equal weight, so the line moves when prices move and not when a different shop happens to get collected. Since a year ago is the reading to trust: it compares two months and stops there. Index, base = 100 instead cumulates every month's move onto one fixed starting month, and that starting month is itself a single thin reading — useful, but only if you know what it is resting on. Price level US$ is a real dollars-per-kilo figure, so it is offered only for a single named item; a dollars-per-kilo of a whole grouping is not a quantity anyone can buy. Because everything is in US dollars, a line can also move because the currency moved — Currency effects splits those two apart. Quiet periods are joined by a dashed line; hover any point to see how much data stands behind it.

How expensive is the basket, country by country?

Matched-basket price level in US$, item by item.
Each country's prices are compared with the world median item by item — 1 kg of rice against 1 kg of rice, 1 litre of milk against 1 litre of milk — and those differences are then averaged. A country at 150 pays roughly 50% more in US dollars for the same shopping basket than the typical country here. An item only counts if most of the countries on this list price it, so the basket is the same basket everywhere rather than whatever each country happened to have collected. Click any bar to open that country.

Does our price level agree with the World Bank’s?

Our matched-basket price level against an external one, world = 100 on both axes.
The World Bank’s International Comparison Program sends enumerators to price a common list of about a thousand products in each economy and works out what a currency really buys. That is the same question this dashboard asks from scraped shelf prices, asked by different people with a different method, so the two ought to land near the dashed diagonal. Where they do, our number is doing its job. Where a country sits far off it, one of the two is wrong — and sometimes it is ours.

This is a benchmark, not a correction: nothing here is folded back into the ranking above.

Why only food and drink? Because that is all this dashboard prices. Every number behind it is a retail shelf listing in COICOP divisions 01 and 02 — food, non-alcoholic beverages, alcohol and tobacco — while the ICP prices the whole of consumption, clothing and transport and rent and schooling included. The scope is matched to ours on purpose: a food-and-drink basket set against a benchmark that also prices rent would look authoritative and mean nothing, and this card exists to be a check rather than to look like one. So the ICP figure here is aggregated over exactly the categories each country actually prices, under exactly the basket weights the ranking is currently using, and the only difference left between the two is the prices themselves. Tobacco sits on neither axis: it is in the corpus, but no tobacco item is priced in enough countries to enter the matched basket, so it is dropped from both sides rather than counted on one. The scope widens when our own prices do.

The whole-economy figures also price rent, healthcare and haircuts, which are cheap in poor countries in a way that food is not — expect them to sit below the line at the bottom of the scale for that reason alone.

Vintage matters here. A price level is a real exchange rate and it moves every year; most benchmark values are from the ICP’s 2021 round while these prices were scraped this year, and a handful of countries are benchmarked as far back as 2011. Each country’s vintage is in its tooltip and in the table.

What makes this country more or less expensive?

Each group’s share of the country’s distance from the world.
A country's overall gap from the world is the sum of what each group contributes. A bar going up means that group is more expensive than the world and big enough in the country's basket to matter; a bar going down means it pulls the country's prices below the world. The last bar is the total. Groups are ordered from the biggest push upward to the biggest pull downward. This one is always against the world, whatever the yardstick above is set to — it is the same decomposition the ranking is built from.

What moved: the currency, or the price?

Each product's own price move, with the shared exchange-rate effect hung off the tip of it.
A US$ price can move for two reasons: the shop changed its price, or the local currency changed against the dollar. The second one is the same for everything in the country — it cannot be bigger for rice than for beer. So the solid bar is the first reason only, the product's own price move with the currency taken out, and that is the part worth comparing across products. The hatched tip on the end of it is the currency, drawn at the same length on every row, and where the tip ends is what the US$ price actually did. When the two pull opposite ways the tip folds back over its own bar, and can cross zero. Figures are in log points so the two parts add up exactly to the total; for small moves a log point and a per cent are much the same number.

How much of a price move is the exchange rate?

Indexed to the first period with data = 100.
When a price in US dollars rises, two different things can be happening: the shop really raised its price, or the local currency lost value against the dollar. The local currency line is the shop; the exchange rate line is the currency. The US$ line is what the two do together.

Where these prices come from

Shelf prices scraped from online retailers, turned into one comparable number per country, category and unit of measure.

  1. Collect
    Product pages are scraped from retail sites and catalogues, plus archived copies of the same pages for earlier periods. Each row is a product name, a price, a currency and a date.
  2. Classify
    Every product name is assigned a COICOP category by a classifier, so the same item lands in the same bucket whatever language or spelling the shop used.
  3. Read the quantity
    The pack size is parsed out of the product name and the price divided by it. A 400 g bag and a 2 kg bag of the same rice both become a price per kilogram. That is the unit value.
  4. Check
    Rows are dropped when the quantity will not parse, the price is impossible, no exchange rate exists, or the value is an outlier inside its own cell. Cells outside absolute plausibility bounds are flagged and kept visible, never silently removed.
  5. Compare
    Countries are compared item by item against the world median for the same category and the same unit, then combined. Units are never pooled with each other.

What this is not

There are no expenditure weights here, so this is a price level, not a cost-of-living index. Coverage differs across countries because of sourcing and language, not because markets differ. Local-currency figures are never comparable across countries.
