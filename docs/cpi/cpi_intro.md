## Introduction

This project explores the use of web-scraped online supermarket prices to measure consumer price dynamics and inflation in data-scarce contexts. Many countries—particularly small island states and developing economies—face constraints in the frequency, coverage, or timeliness of traditional price collection systems. By leveraging publicly available online retail data, this project investigates whether high-frequency supermarket prices can provide useful signals on consumer price evolution and complement official statistics.

The main objective is to construct experimental Consumer Price Indices (CPIs) and related price indicators using harmonized price microdata collected from online supermarkets across multiple countries. The project is explicitly exploratory and research-oriented: it does not aim to replace official CPI production, but rather to assess the feasibility, limitations, and analytical value of web-based price data for economic measurement.

The project currently covers online supermarkets in Australia, Fiji, Papua New Guinea, Tonga, Vanuatu, Japan, Indonesia, the Philippines, and Samoa. Prices are collected on a weekly basis by crawling retailer category pages down to individual product pages. When available, historical price observations are retrieved using the Internet Archive’s Wayback Machine, allowing partial reconstruction of past price dynamics.

The unit of observation is a product–store–date price. Raw prices are standardized through unit normalization to account for heterogeneous package sizes, multi-pack offers, and mixed units of measurement, enabling the computation of comparable unit values (e.g. price per kilogram or liter). Products are mapped to international consumption categories using an LLM-assisted classification approach aligned with the COICOP framework.

The analytical component focuses on price relatives, inflation rates, descriptive price statistics, and experimental index construction using standard formulas such as the Jevons and Young indices, with expenditure weights sourced from National Statistics Offices where available. Results are benchmarked against official CPI and inflation estimates from National Statistical Offices and the IMF. Key limitations—such as the exclusion of informal markets, the online-offline price gap, and limited geographic resolution—are explicitly acknowledged.
